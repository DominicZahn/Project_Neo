\section{Fundamentals} \label{sec:fundamentals}
During this section a brief overview of fundamental concepts in rigid body dynamics, dynamic stability and optimal control is given.
We will focus on concepts concepts used in the later stages of the thesis, so they can be referenced.
The goal is not to give an exhaustive explanation of these formulations, but provide the reader with a high-level understanding.
Through out each subject we will recommend suitable literature for in-depth research.
\\

\subsection{Rigid Body Dynamics} \label{sec:fundamentals:rigid}
The following equations are taken from Featherstones 2008 "Rigid Body Dynamics Algorithms" Book.
These are also the fundamentals for the algorithms implemented in for example Pinocchio \cite{pinocchioweb,carpentier2019pinocchio} and \acrfull{rbdl} \cite{Felis2016}.
\\
In its book Featherstone describes rigid body systems as collection of bodies connected by joints.
Each body is considered rigid so not able to change its shape and inertia like soft bodies would.
The whole rigid body system may also stand under the influence of various forces, while the joints restrict the resulting motions of the bodies.

We can describe the context between force and motion of a rigid body with the equations of motions.

\paragraph{Equations of Motions}
When a single rigid body $B$ is experiencing the force $\bm f$ its motion is described by
\begin{equation}
    \bm f = M \bm a + \bm p \label{eq:rbd:motionEq:rigid},
\end{equation}
where the inertia $M$ and bias force $\bm p$ are characteristics of the body and $a$ the resulting acceleration.
For an articulated body of multiple rigid bodies the equation is analogue to the one of a single body:
\begin{equation}
    \bm f = M^A \bm a + \bm p^A \label{eq:rbd:motionEq:articulated}
\end{equation}
The difference lies only in the inertia $I^A$ and bias force $\bm p^A$, which now also include the ones from $B'$.
Through usage of generalized forces $\tau$ instead of $\bm f$, as well as adding joint angle dependency, we specialize \cref{eq:rbd:motionEq:articulated} to the classical formulation of the equation of motions:
\begin{equation}
  \bm \tau = M(\bm q)\bm{\ddot q}+C(\bm q, \bm{\dot q}) \label{eq:rbd:motionEq:general}
\end{equation}
In \cref{eq:rbd:motionEq:general} $\bm q$ represents the joint angles, with joint velocities $\bm{\dot q}$ and accelerations $\bm{\ddot q}$.
$M(\bm q)$ denotes as the generalized inertia matrix of the rigid body system.
As the relative positioning of the rigid bodies towards each other changes the overall inertia of the system, it depends on the joint angle positions $\bm q$.
The generalized bias force is renamed to $C(\bm q, \bm{\dot q})$, in order to better fit its representation for Coriolis and centrifugal forces, gravity, and any other forces acting on the system that are not coming from the generalized force vector $\bm \tau$.
Because of that the generalized bias force is also a function of the joint angles $\bm q$ and velocities $\bm{\dot q}$.
\\
This results in \cref{eq:rbd:motionEq:general} being constructed from the variables $\bm q$ and $\bm \tau$, as well as the coefficients $M$ and $C$.

\paragraph{Forward Dynamics and Inverse Dynamics}
Based on the previously formulated generalized equation of motion (\cref{eq:rbd:motionEq:general}) we are able to calculate either the generalized forces $\bm \tau$ or joint velocities $\bm{\dot q}$.
As those describe the dynamics of our system, it is either called forward or inverse dynamics.
Inverse dynamics calculates the resulting generalized forces from joint angles, velocities and accelerations, it can be solved with the \acrfull{rnea}.
To find the produced joint accelerations, for an articulated body with $\bm \tau$ the problem of forward dynamics has to be solved.

However in forward dynamics not only joint accelerations, but also joint constraint forces are unknown.
As a result we are using propagation methods to calculate coefficients locally and propagate them to their neighbors.
The fastest method is the \acrfull{aba}, with a complexity of $O(n_B)$ if $n_B$ is the amount of bodies in a kinematic tree.
\\
The algorithm is split into three passes to calculate:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item velocities and bias terms (Outward Pass),
  \item group inertias and bias forces (Inward Pass) and
  \item accelerations (Outward Pass)
\end{enumerate}
During the first pass we starting from the root of the kinematic tree and move towards the leafs.
It calculates the local velocities and bias forces for each body $B_i$.
The next pass computes from the leafs towards the root the in \cref{fig:rbd:featherstone:rbd:aba_articulatedBodies} shown articulated bodies $A_i$ inertia and bias force.
For a final pass the algorithm moves again outwards, building on top of the previously calculated elements to find the accelerations.
\\
For a detailed rundown of the algorithm and its equations we recommend chapter 7.3 in Featherstones book.

\subsection{Contact Formulation} \label{sec:fundamentals:contacts}
Contact Formulations are an important part in robotics and can be modelled in different forms.
While there are soft contacts, that handle connections between, for example a robots feet and the ground as a spring-damper system, it is also common to find rigid contacts as a solution.
As our overarching application is a humanoid, that performs dynamic high force inducing motions, we opt for rigid contacts.
For stepping, this choice would lead us to a multi-phase model, as the solution connects rigidly to the ground.
However, as we are planning to keep the robot in a bipedal stance, this is not the case.
\\
Generally rigid body contacts can be formulated a constraint equations of motion, which is solved during constrained dynamics.

\paragraph{Constrained Dynamics}
The following equations and methods can be found in \cite{carpentier_2021}, which describes the algorithms used to handle constrained dynamics in the rigid body dynamics library Pinocchio \cite{pinocchioweb}.
\\
Carpentier et al. states that the constraint equations of motion follow the least-action, or Maupertuis principle.
It declares, that the acceleration of the system $\bm{\ddot q}$ should as close as possible to the free-falling case $\bm{\ddot q}_\text{free}$ \cite{carpentier_2021} in terms of the kinematic metric.
As a consequence the principle can be written as following optimization problem: 
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:consDyn:default}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\bm {\ddot q}(\cdot)} \quad & \frac 1 2 \| \bm{\ddot q} - \bm{\ddot q_\text{free}}(\bm q, \bm{\dot q}, \bm \tau) \|^2_M \label{eq:consDyn:default:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & f_c(\bm q) = 0 \label{eq:consDyn:default:cons}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
The kinematic metric used by Carpentier et al. is a norm with respect to the mass-inertia matrix $M$.
\Cref{eq:consDyn:default:cons} shows the implicit constraint function $f_C$, which forces for example a foot to stay at a specific ground position.
\\
We derive the constraint \cref{eq:consDyn:default:cons} two times, which introduces the Jacobian $J_{f_c}$ and its derivative, and forms:
\begin{equation}
  J_{f_c}(\bm{q})\,\bm{\dot q} + \underbrace{\dot{J}_{f_c}(\bm{q},\bm{\dot q})\,\bm{\dot q}}_{\gamma_{f_c}(\bm{q},\bm{\dot q})} = 0
  \label{eq:consDyn:default:jacobian}
\end{equation}
The second term $\gamma_{f_c}(\bm{q},\bm{\dot q})$ can be understood as acceleration drift.
\Cref{eq:consDyn:default:obj,eq:consDyn:default:jacobian} combine to an equality-constraint quadratic program.
As this is a well-known problem class we can skip right to the \acrfull{kkt} condition:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
  \mathbf{0} & J_{f_c} \\
  J_{f_c}^{t} & M
  \end{bmatrix}}_{K(\bm{q})}
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \bm \lambda \\
  \bm{\ddot q}
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
  -\gamma_{f_c} \\
  M \bm{\ddot q}_{\mathrm{free}}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:consDyn:default:KKT}
\end{equation}
Solving it leads us right to the optimum of our original constraint equation of motion \cref{eq:consDyn:default}.
$M$ represents the mass-inertia matrix, while $K(\bm q)$ is the so-called \acrfull{kkt} matrix.
In an ideal world it is symmetrical positive definite so we are able to solve \cref{eq:consDyn:default:KKT} with for example the Cholesky Decomposition.
This saddle point is than holding the contact forces $\bm \lambda$ and acceleration $\bm{\ddot q}$.
For cases where the \acrshort{kkt} matrix is not symmetrical positive definite, we need to find different solutions.

\paragraph{Ill-conditioned Jacobian $J_{f_c}$}
The desired method for solving the \acrshort{kkt} condition \cref{eq:consDyn:default:KKT} is by using the Cholesky Decomposition.
A requirement for this method is a symmetrical positive definite \acrshort{kkt} matrix $K(\bm q)$.
For positive mass $M$ can be considered as symmetrical positive definite.
The Jacobian $J_{f_c}$ however can reach rank deficient states, when the robots configuration uses redundant contacts or reaches singularities.
\\
Therefore it is not always possible to find the inverse of the \acrshort{kkt} matrix.
Carpentier et al. mentions, that there are different generic methods to solve it numerically.
However none of these is specifically catered towards explicitly exploiting the typical structure of the matrix.
\par
To fill this gap they propose in \cite{carpentier_2021} an alternative method, which is based on the idea of proximal algorithms \cite{parikh_2014}.
Those are typically used to solve nonsmooth, constrained, large-scale or distributed optimization problems \cite{parikh_2014}.
The heart of each proximal algorithm is a proximal operator of the form:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{prox}_{f,\alpha}(y) \overset{\text{def}}{=} \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \; f(x) + \frac{1}{2\alpha} \|x - y\|_2^2
  \label{eq:consDyn:prox:operator}
\end{equation}
It still searches for the minimal value $x$ of the function $f(\bm x)$, but does so on a smooth variant.
Through the addition of the second term the proximal operator ensures the smoothness of the function, but changes the resulting minimum.
To remove that perturbation the proximal operator is used in the recursion:
\begin{equation}
  x_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{f,\alpha}(x_k)
  \label{eq:consDyn:prox:algo}
\end{equation}
$\alpha$ is used as a step size, while a tolerance or maximum iteration count stop the algorithm at a sufficient approximation of the optimal value.
\par
In \cite{carpentier_2021} Carpentier et al. apply the proximal algorithm concept to finding the solution of an ill-conditioned \acrshort{kkt} condition \cref{eq:consDyn:default:KKT}:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
  \alpha I_m & J_{f_c} \\
  J_{f_c}^{t} & M
  \end{bmatrix}}_{K(\bm{q})}
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \bm{\lambda} \\
  \bm{\ddot{q}}
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
  -\gamma_{f_c}- \alpha I_m \\
  M \bm{\ddot q}_{\mathrm{free}}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:consDyn:prox:KKT}
\end{equation}
The claim to reach sufficient results for even small iteration counts and provide it as an algorithm, to calculate contact forces and accelerations in there rigid body dynamics library Pinocchio \cite{pinocchioweb,carpentier2019pinocchio}.

\subsection{Dynamic Stability} \label{sec:fundamentals:dynamicStability}
For legged and especially bipedal robotics stability is a fundamental property.
At first the definition of a stable motion seems as simple as, "do not tip over", but when it comes to quantifying it the criteria are differing and full of drawbacks.
\\
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{figures/02_fundamentals/bioInspired/dynPoints.pdf}
  \caption{Multiple different stability criteria, which are sketched out for an exemplary human working motion \cite{sharbafi_2017}.}
  \label{fig:dynStab:points}
\end{figure}
The most intuitive and oldest solution is the ground projected \acrfull{com} (GCoM).
As visualized in \cref{fig:dynStab:points} it is a direct projection of the \acrshort{com} onto the ground.
Is it inside the region of support or also called \acrfull{pos}, is the pose considered statically stable.
However as the name already suggests, this solely works for static poses.
During for example walking motions the GCoM regularly leaves the \acrshort{pos} and still the robot is not falling.
\par
This is were dynamic stability comes into play.
Therefore we shift our focus to dynamic properties of the walking motions.
Mombaur and Vallery describe all of the points shown in \cref{fig:dynStab:points} in a sub-chapter of \cite{sharbafi_2017}.
They also go into more detail, how each point is computed and can be used to realize for example a walking controller.
In this section we are restricting ourselves to the three most common ones.
\\
If we think about for example situations, where humans are pushed from a bipedal stance one of their immediate reactions will be a step to stop the momentum and come to a hold.
The exact point at which the person places their foot can be described by the \acrfull{cp} \cite{pratt_2006}.
To compute it we model the human as a linear inverted pendulum and solve the differential equation.
For a \acrshort{cp} inside the \acrshort{pos} there is no need to take a step, so the robot or human is considered stable.
\\
Another important point is the \acrfull{cop}.
It is the point, where all of the distributed forces across contact patch can be expressed by a single equivalent force \cite{kajita_2014}.
In the case of nonsliding motions, level ground and no external forces, apart form ground reaction forces, it is equivalent to the \acrfull{zmp} \cite{vukobratovic_1990}.
While both describe the same location during these conditions they are computed from different sources.
The \acrshort{cop} results from external forces, while the \acrshort{zmp} is defined by the moment $M^{gi}$, that is induced by gravity, acceleration and change of angular momentum in the segments, as well as the sum of gravity and inertia forces at the \acrshort{com}.
As a result the \acrshort{zmp} is computed from the internal properties and accelerations, in contrast to the more externally focused \acrshort{cop}.
The \acrshort{zmp} can be formulated as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \bm p_\text{ZMP} = \frac{\bm n \times M^{gi}}{R_{gi} \cdot \bm n}
  \label{eq:dynStab:ZMP}
\end{equation}
Besides $R_{gi}$ and $M^{gi}$ the normal vector $\bm n$ has to be known in order to calculate the position of the \acrshort{zmp}.
\\
Kajita et al. list three limitations of the \acrshort{zmp}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item not able to determine slipping
  \item none flat ground surfaces
  \item additional contacts using the hands
\end{itemize}
\par
Through out this section we gave a rough overview about some legged robots stability metrics.
For the interested reader, we recommend "Stability and Robustness of Bipedal Walking" \cite{sharbafi_2017} as well as "Introduction to Humanoid Robotics" \cite{kajita_2014} as more indepth works in the field of walking control.
General system stability criteria like Lyapunov Stability \cite{lyapunov_1992} were diligently left out to put focus on legged motion specific ones, like the \acrshort{zmp}.

\subsection{Optimal Control} \label{sec:fundamentals:optimalControl}
Optimal Control is used in multiple different areas, controlling legged robots, or chemical processes.
It can come to use, whenever an dynamic system needs to be optimized.
At least in robotics its most popular applications are in real-time control or motion generation.
\\
During this section we are closely following the philosophy and implementation of Acados \cite{verschueren_2021}, as this is the software will are using in this project.
A general non-linear optimal control problem can be formulated in the form:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:oc:noc}
  \begin{align}
    & \qquad \textbf{Objective Function} \nonumber \\
    \min_{\bm x(\cdot),\bm u(\cdot)} \quad
      & \int_0^{T_f} l\left(\bm x(t), \bm u(t)\right) dt
      + m(\bm x(T_f)) \label{eq:oc:noc:obj} \\
    % Dynamics
    & \qquad \textbf{System Dynamics} \nonumber \\
    \textrm{s.t.} \quad & \bm{\dot x}(t) = f(\bm x(t),\bm u(t)), & t \in \left[0,T_f \right], \label{eq:oc:noc:dyn} \\
    % Equality Constraints
    & \qquad \textbf{Equality Constraint} \nonumber \\
    & 0 \leq g(\bm x(t),\bm u(t)), & t \in \left[0,T_f \right], \label{eq:oc:noc:eqCons} \\
    % Inequality Constraints
    & \qquad \textbf{Inequality Constraint} \nonumber \\
    & 0 = h(\bm x(t),\bm u(t)), & t \in \left[0,T_f \right]. \label{eq:oc:noc:ineqCons}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
The problem is defined by the dynamics $f(\bm x(t),u(t))$, states $\bm x(t)$ and controls $\bm u(t)$ of the system.
\Cref{eq:oc:noc:obj} is the objective function for which the states and controls will be optimized, this can for example be the total energy of the system.
Additionally it equality (\cref{eq:oc:noc:eqCons}) and inequality (\cref{eq:oc:noc:eqCons}) constraints can be formulated.
The previously mentioned \acrshort{zmp} could be limited to a shrunken \acrshort{pos} to ensure dynamic stability, with an inequality constraint.
\\
As the problem is described over a duration $\left[0,T_f\right]$, time, states and controls are continuous.
Controls are mostly discretized through piecewise polynomials or constants.
To transfer the states $\bm x$ there are in general two classes of approaches: direct and indirect.
Direct methods first discretize and than optimize, while indirect choose an inverse idea.
Indirect methods are therefore a lot harder to solve and are mostly used as ground-truth of direct methods.
\\
A popular direct solution direct multiple shooting \cite{bock_1984}, where the states are discretized by forward simulating with the given controls to the next state over the time span.

\subsubsection{Multiple Shooting} \label{sec:fundamentals:optimalControl:multipleShooting}
Direct shooting methods introduce a discrete time grid $t_k$, which leads to discrete states $\bm x_k$.
The idea is to "shoot" from state $\bm x_k$ to the next one using the piecewise defined control $\bm u_k$ to simulate with the forward integration:
\begin{equation}
  \bm x_{k+1} = \bm \Phi_k(\bm x_k, \bm u_k), \quad k=0,\dots,N-1
  \label{eq:oc:forwardInt}
\end{equation}
Despite the differing naming schema, \cref{fig:oc:multiple} from Gros and Diehl \cite{gros_}, displays the bespoken intervals, controls and states well.
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/02_fundamentals/nocses/multiple.pdf}
  \caption{This example of a multiple shooting approach was taken from \cite{gros_}. The piecewise constant controls here $\bm q_k$ are used to forward integrate the states and create a discrete \acrfull{nlp}, like \cref{eq:oc:multiple}.}
  \label{fig:oc:multiple}
\end{figure}
To be precise the in \cref{fig:oc:multiple} visualized approach is direct multiple shooting.
In contrast to direct single shooting it uses multiple intervals over which the method is optimizing simultaneous.
Single shooting is fully sequential and tends to diverge for long integration times.
This long time span is cut down by introducing more smaller intervals, which are required to be connected.
These connections are added as equality constraints.
The general multiple shooting method was first found by Brock and Plitt \cite{bock_1984}.
\\
After applying direct multiple shooting to \cref{eq:oc:noc}, we achieve the discrete \acrshort{nlp} formulation:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:oc:multiple}
  \begin{align}
    & \qquad \textbf{Objective Function} \nonumber \\
    \min_{\substack{\bm x_0, \dots, \bm x_N, \\ \bm u_0, \dots, \bm u_{N-1}}} \quad
    & \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (t_{k+1}-t_k) \cdot l\left(\bm x_k, \bm u_k\right) dt
      + m(\bm x_N) \label{eq:oc:multiple:obj} \\
    % Dynamics
    & \qquad \textbf{System Dynamics} \nonumber \\
    \textrm{s.t.} \quad & \bm x_{k+1} = \bm \Phi(\bm x_k, \bm u_k), & k = 0,\dots,N-1, \label{eq:oc:multiple:dyn} \\
    % Equality Constraints
    & \qquad \textbf{Equality Constraint} \nonumber \\
    & 0 \leq g_k(\bm x_k,\bm u_k), & k=1,\dots,N-1, \label{eq:oc:multiple:eqCons} \\
    % Inequality Constraints
    & \qquad \textbf{Inequality Constraint} \nonumber \\
    & 0 = h(\bm x_k,\bm u_k), & k=1,\dots,N-1. \label{eq:oc:multiple:ineqCons}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Such problems are now solvable with standard \acrfull{nlp} solvers like \acrfull{ipopt} \cite{wachter_2006} or specifically catered ones, that abuse the structure of the system.
Acados does this, by using an \acrfull{sqp} algorithm to break the solution up into a cascade of smaller \acrfull{qp} formulations, which are easier to solve.
