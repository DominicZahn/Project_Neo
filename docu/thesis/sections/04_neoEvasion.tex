\section{Neo} \label{sec:neo}
In the last section we identified the dodge constraint \cref{eq:neo:constraints:dodge} as a major shortcoming of our \textit{Morpheus} method.
It limits solely the last pose of the motion, so that the approaching object could collide in the time during the motion.
Another problem are approaching object with steep collision angles.
Those objects can not be avoided by the robot ducking underneath a certain height, as the exact end pose and positioning of the body is important.
\\
The solution is the usage of a safety distance, that should be kept all the time between the robot and the approaching object.
Using the exact distance between object and robot body also enable the solver to find minimal evasion motions, where the robot perturbs away from the object, without the need for spacious or unstable motions.
The next step is therefore to improve our previous formulation by inclusion of a more detailed dodge constraint
This will form a descendant of the \textit{Morpheus} motion generator called \textit{Neo}.
\par
At first we are tackling the challenge of computing the distance between robot and approaching object (\cref{sec:neo:distance}).
Afterwards the formulation is adjusted to better suit the changes made in the dodge constraint.
Like in the previous section we conclude with an analysis of a single motion, before thoroughly testing the method in \cref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Robot Object Distance} \label{sec:neo:distance}
The field of separation-distance calculation is closely tied to collision detection and avoidance.
To effectively plane motions for collision avoidance distance between objects must be computed \cite{toth_2017}.
\\
During the last decades multiple different solutions were developed.
One of them is the DCOL algorithm from Tracy et al. \cite{tracy_2023}.
They formulate a convex optimization problem with a conic constraint, that scales both objects until they create a minimal overlap.
The distance as well as the closest points to each other are computed by finding the overlap point on the respective object.
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/04_neoEvasion/tracy_scaled.pdf}
  \caption{
    The image shows the principle of the DCOL algorithm from Tracy et al. \cite{tracy_2023}.
  Both objects are scaled until they intersect to compute the closest points, while implicitly calculating the distance between them.
}
  \label{fig:neo:distance:tracy:scaled}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:neo:distance:tracy:scaled} displays an example were a cone and polytope are scaled until they meet in the red marked point.
By rescaling this intersection to the original volumes surface the closest points on both objects can be found.
\\
Building a full motion planner around such methods leads to bi-level formulations, where two levels of optimization need to be solved.
In every iteration of the motion generator, the lower level separation-distance has to be computed.
Especially for a system, where we use analytical derivations this setup poses problems, as the solver would like to have the separation distance as a mathematical expression.
\\
A solution are analytically defined signed distance functions like the "Minimum-Offset-To-Touch" (MOTT) from 
Jaitly et al. \cite{jaitly_2025}.
It defines the distance between two objects by calculating the minimal offset of an object towards another before they overlap.
Liu et al. \cite{liu_2023} also showcase the abilities of the \acrfull{sdf}.
They propose a system that uses neural networks to learn the \acrshort{sdf} of a complex shape.
With some further adjustments this lands itself well towards embedding it inside a stochastic optimization motion generator.
Both result in a single level problem formulation, as the distance can be computed directly, without solving a nested separation-distance optimization.
\\
To formulate the dodge constraint in our \textit{Neo} solution, we therefore chose \acrshort{sdf} for modelling our robot to object distance.
\par
Let's take a step back and quickly understand what an \acrshort{sdf} is about.
So called distance functions define, for each point in space, the distance to a specified object \cite{jones_2006}.
As the name already suggests, \textit{signed} distance functions differentiate themselves from normal distance function by providing a direction.
They are for example defined as negative inside the described shape, while retaining positive values outside of it.
We will stick with that notation for the rest of this work.
\par
A \acrshort{sdf} functions easy to evaluate and compose properties show themselves when used in path-planning for robotic arms \cite{zhu_2025}, autonomous navigation of \acrshort{uav}s \cite{jacquet_2025} or dexterous manipulation \cite{yang_2025}. \\
Our work leverages a \acrshort{sdf} for the collision hull of our humanoid robot, it provides the distance between the robot and an approaching object $d(\bm{p}(t))$ for every point in time $t$.
The following section presents the collision model for the upper body of our H1-2
It is composed of ellipsoid-shaped collision primitives, which are fused together with a smooth transition function.

\subsubsection{Ellipsoid Collision Primitives}
To generate the collision hull for the Unitree H1-2 we chose ellipsoids as collision primitives.
One advantage of ellipsoids over other collision shapes is the ability to seamlessly fit them onto a known geometry.
John-Löwner Ellipsoids are a well-known solution for such problems \cite{john_1985}.
Other high-level methods have been found, like ellipsoid decompositions from meshes \cite{bischoff_kobbelt_2009} or optimization-based solutions \cite{elkhoury_2013}.
\\
Other suitable shapes like boxes, capsules or rounded cones come with non differential functions.
In contrast the \acrshort{sdf} definition
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid}
  \begin{align}
    k_0 &= \left\lVert \frac{\bm{p} - \bm{c}}{\bm r} \right\rVert_2 \\
    k_1 &= \left\lVert \frac{\bm{p} - \bm{c}}{\bm r^2} \right\rVert_2 \\
    d(\bm{p}) &= \frac{k_0 \cdot (k_0-1)}{k_1},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
if smooth and differentiable at every point.
The from Quilez \cite{sdfEllipsoid} presented \cref{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid}, is a bounded approximation, as there is no exact analytical solution for triaxial ellipsoids.
However, as the solution is zero bound on the surface, and has a good approximation of the euclidean distance, its differentiable property make it most suitable primitive for a simple to define \acrshort{sdf}.
For true high fidelity collision hull other more complex \acrshort{sdf} construction methods are advised.

In our case fitting ellipsoids like shown in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives} is a close enough approximation.
Each of the primitives is anchored to a local frame of the robot.
Three of them are placed on each arm, one on the torso and another one in the head area.

\subsubsection{Smooth Transition Function}
The intuitive solution to compute the minimal distance to a set of objects, like the one displayed in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives} is to build a proximity graph, for example a Voroni diagram.
The distance between a point in space and the shortest distance the set then requires the search on this structure \cite{toth_2017}.
Especially for volumes like the ones displayed in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives}, the primitives can be fused to a single \acrshort{sdf}, which removes the necessity of the proximity graph.
To keep the derivative of the distance function smooth, a suitable transition function is required.
\par
One of those is the sigmoid function
\begin{equation}
\text{smin}_k(d_i,d_j) = d_i+\frac{d_j-d_i}{1-e^{\frac{d_j-d_i}{k\log 2}}} = d_\text{union} \label{eq:neo:distance:smin},
\end{equation}
which was proposed by Quilez for fusing multiple \acrshort{sdf}s to a single surface \cite{sdfSmoothMin}.
\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/04_neoEvasion/surferBoy.png}
  \caption{
    The application of \cref{eq:neo:distance:smin} to render a face composed of multiple different shapes.
    The illustration was taken from Quilez \cite{sdfSmoothMin}.
  }
  \label{fig:neo:sdf:face}
\end{figure}
As displayed in \cref{fig:neo:sdf:face} Quilez used this technique in raymarching to blend a multitude of different shapes to smoothly rendered face.
\\
The smoothness can be described by the factor $k$ in \cref{eq:neo:distance:smin}.
As we are step-by-step concatenating primitives together, we also would be able to set individual smoothing factors at each connection.
Because each of the ellipsoids is anchored to a local frame of the robot, the connections between them adapt for towards pose changes.
The result of this method is a human like skin, that spans from one ellipsoid to another, generating a continuous collision hull (\cref{fig:neo:distance:composed}).
To visualize \acrshort{sdf}s, like the collision hull in \cref{fig:neo:distance:composed}, we used the the marching cube implementation of Lewiner et al. \cite{lewiner_2003}.
\par
In this section we derived a closed from formulation for calculating the distance from an artificial point in space to the \acrshort{sdf}-based collision hull.
The formulations derivative is smooth and provides a good approximation for the euclidean distance.
At this point we got all the components necessary to adjust the previous \textit{Morpheus} formulation \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints}.

\subsection{Optimal Control Formulation Adjustments}
The core formulation for \textit{Neo} is the same as for \textit{Morpheus}.
We are keeping the model from \cref{sec:morpheus:model} and the use the same controls, states, and dynamics as in \cref{eq:morpheus:dyn,eq:morpheus:constraints:f}.
The overarching goal is to remain in a bipedal stance, while avoiding an approaching object.
\\
The first step is to update the in \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints:dodge} given dodge constraint
\begin{equation}
  d_\text{safe} \leq d(\bm q)
  \label{eq:neo:constraints:dodge},
\end{equation}
while $d(\bm p)$ is the fused \acrshort{sdf} of the collision hull, we constructed with \cref{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid,eq:neo:distance:smin}.
As the robot is now tracking the full trajectory of the object we are able to move the robot not only into the evasion pose, but also consider the following recovery.
To test this new ability, the objects trajectory is considered to pass the robot at around have distance, so that a combined evasion recovery motion is calculated.
As a result of the improved evasion scenario we are also updating the terminal constraint to
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm q - q_\epsilon \\
    - v_\text{stat.}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \leq \bm x_f \leq
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm q + q_\epsilon \\
    v_\text{stat.},
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
which is based on \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints:terminal}.
Instead of solely requiring a quasi-stable end position, we add the initial joint position to the constraint.
\\
Another change is done in the composed objective function:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\bm x(\cdot),\bm u(\cdot)} \quad
  \int_0^{T_f} \left(\omega_q l_q(\bm q) + \omega_r l_r(\bm{u}) \right) dt
\end{equation}
As the motion has to be a longer to cover evasion and recovery, we need to make sure that the solver does not "park" the robot in an unnatural stability optimum.
A better solution is to hold the robot as long as possible in the current position mimicking a robot, working at a task, and moving its body in the last moment out of the way.
The consequence is the removal of the stability $l_s$ and head term $m_h$ in favor of a new joint pose Lagrange term:
\begin{equation}
    l_q(\bm x) = \frac{\| \bm q-\bm q_0 \|^2_2}{(n_q \cdot \bm{q}_\text{exp}^2)^2}
    \label{eq:neo:obj:joint}
\end{equation}
These changes to \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints} form our new \textit{Neo} formulation.
For the next section we are taking a look at the resulting motion for an object that is targeted towards the robots head.
