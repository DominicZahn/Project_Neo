In the last section we identified the dodge constraint \cref{eq:neo:constraints:dodge} as a major shortcoming of our \textit{Morpheus} method.
During the swing up motion, the approaching object  able to hit the robot before it reaches its final pose.
Another problem are approaching objects with steep collision angles.
Those objects can not be avoided by the robot ducking underneath a certain height, as the end pose and positioning of the upper body has to be adjusted accordingly.
\\
The solution is a safety distance that should be kept all the time between the robot and the approaching object.
Calculating the distance between object and robot body also enables the generator to find minimal evasion motions, where the robot perturbs away from the object, without the need for spacious or unstable motions.
The next step is therefore to improve our previous formulation by inclusion of a more detailed dodge constraint.
This will form a descendant of the \textit{Morpheus} motion generator we call \textit{Neo}.
\par
We start of by tackling the challenge of estimating the distance between robot and approaching object (\cref{sec:neo:distance}).
Afterwards the formulation is adjusted to better suit the changes made in the dodge constraint.
Like in the previous section we conclude with an analysis of a single motion (\cref{sec:neo:result}), before thoroughly testing the method in \cref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Robot Object Distance} \label{sec:neo:distance}
The field of separation-distance calculation is closely tied to collision detection and avoidance.
To effectively plan motions for collision avoidance, distance functions towards objects have to be computed \cite{toth_2017}.
\\
During the last decades multiple different solutions were developed.
One of them is the DCOL algorithm from Tracy et al. \cite{tracy_2023}.
They formulate a convex optimization problem with a conic constraint, that scales both objects until they create a minimal overlap.
The distance as well as the closest points to each other are computed by finding the overlap point on the respective object.
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/04_neoEvasion/tracy_scaled.pdf}
  \caption{
    The image shows the principle of the DCOL algorithm from Tracy et al. \cite{tracy_2023}.
  Both objects are scaled until they intersect to compute the closest points, while implicitly calculating the distance between them.
}
  \label{fig:neo:distance:tracy:scaled}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:neo:distance:tracy:scaled} displays an example were a cone and polytope are scaled until they meet in the red marked point.
By rescaling this intersection to the original volumes surface the closest points on both objects can be found.
\\
Building a full motion planner around such methods leads to bi-level formulations, where two levels of optimization need to be solved.
In every iteration of the motion generator, the lower level separation-distance has to be computed.
Especially for a system, where we use analytical derivations this setup poses problems, as the solver would like to have the separation distance as a mathematical expression.
\\
So called distance functions define, for each point in space, the distance to a specified object \cite{jones_2006}.
As the name already suggests, \textit{signed} distance functions differentiate themselves from normal distance function by providing a direction.
They are for example defined as negative inside the described shape, while retaining positive values outside of it.
We will stick with that notation for the rest of this work.
A \acrfull{sdf} functions easy to evaluate and compose properties show themselves when used in path-planning for robotic arms \cite{zhu_2025}, autonomous navigation of \acrshort{uav}s \cite{jacquet_2025} or dexterous manipulation \cite{yang_2025}.
\\
An application of such analytically defined solutions is the "Minimum-Offset-To-Touch" (MOTT) constraint from Jaitly et al. \cite{jaitly_2025}.
It defines the distance between two objects by calculating the minimal offset of an object towards another before they overlap.
Liu et al. \cite{liu_2023} also showcase the abilities of the \acrshort{sdf}.
They propose a system that uses neural networks to learn the \acrshort{sdf} of a complex shape.
With some further adjustments this lands itself well towards embedding it inside a stochastic optimization motion generator.
Both result in a single level problem formulation, as the distance can be computed directly, without solving a nested separation-distance optimization.
\par
As in the case of our optimal object avoidance motion generator, a distance from an arbitary point to a collision hull is required.
We therefore can a avoid the complex field of shape to shape distance computation, of which DCOL is an example.
Our work leverages a \acrshort{sdf} for the collision hull of our humanoid robot.
It provides the distance between the robot and an approaching object $d(\bm{p}(t))$ for every point in time $t$.
\\
The following section presents the collision model for the upper body of our H1-2.
It is composed of ellipsoid-shaped collision primitives, which are fused together with a smooth transition function.

\subsubsection{Ellipsoid Collision Primitives} \label{sec:neo:distance:primitives}
To generate the collision hull for the Unitree H1-2 we chose ellipsoids as collision primitives.
One advantage of ellipsoids over other collision shapes is the ability to seamlessly fit them onto a known geometry.
John-Löwner Ellipsoids are a well-known solution for such problems \cite{john_1985}.
Other high-level methods have been found, like ellipsoid decompositions from meshes \cite{bischoff_kobbelt_2009} or optimization-based solutions \cite{elkhoury_2013}.
\\
Shapes like boxes, capsules or rounded cones come with non differential functions.
In contrast, the \acrshort{sdf} definition of an ellipsoid
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid}
  \begin{align}
    k_0 &= \left\lVert \frac{\bm{p} - \bm{c}}{\bm r} \right\rVert_2 \\
    k_1 &= \left\lVert \frac{\bm{p} - \bm{c}}{\bm r^2} \right\rVert_2 \\
    d(\bm{p}) &= \frac{k_0 \cdot (k_0-1)}{k_1}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
is smooth and differentiable at every point.
\Cref{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid} presented by Quilez, is a bounded approximation, as there is no exact analytical solution for triaxial ellipsoids.
However, as the solution is zero bound on the surface, and has a good approximation of the euclidean distance, its differentiable property make it the most suitable primitive for a simple to define \acrshort{sdf}.
For true high fidelity collision hulls other more complex \acrshort{sdf} construction methods are advised.

\\
In our case fitting ellipsoids manually like shown in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives} is a close enough approximation.
Each of the primitives is anchored to a local frame of the robot.
Three of them are placed on each arm, one on the torso and another one in the head area.
The result is the coverage of the whole upper body, with individual segments, that adjust to pose changes.
In our next step we are fusing the singular primitives to a single \acrshort{sdf}.

\subsubsection{Smooth Transition Function} \label{sec:neo:distance:transition}
The intuitive solution to compute the minimal distance to a set of objects, like the one displayed in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives} is to build a proximity graph, for example a Voroni diagram.
In order to get the shortest distance between a point in space and the set of objects is to search the Voroni diagram \cite{toth_2017}.
Especially for volumes like the ones displayed in \cref{fig:neo:distance:primitives}, the primitives can be fused to a single \acrshort{sdf}, which removes the necessity of the proximity graph.
To keep the derivative of the distance function smooth, a suitable transition function is required.
\par
One of those transition functions is the sigmoid function
\begin{equation}
\text{smin}_k(d_i,d_j) = d_i+\frac{d_j-d_i}{1-e^{\frac{d_j-d_i}{k\log 2}}} = d_\text{union} \label{eq:neo:distance:smin},
\end{equation}
which was proposed by Quilez for fusing multiple \acrshort{sdf}s to a unified one \cite{sdfSmoothMin}.
\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/04_neoEvasion/surferBoy.png}
  \caption{
    Quilez \cite{sdfSmoothMin} application of \cref{eq:neo:distance:smin} to render a face composed of multiple different shapes.
  }
  \label{fig:neo:sdf:face}
\end{figure}
As displayed in \cref{fig:neo:sdf:face} Quilez used this technique in raymarching to blend a multitude of different shapes to a smoothly rendered face.
\\
The smoothness can be described by the factor $k$ in \cref{eq:neo:distance:smin}.
As we are step-by-step concatenating primitives together, we also would be able to set individual smoothing factors at each connection.
Because each of the ellipsoids is anchored to a local frame of the robot, the connections between them adapt for changes in the pose.
The result of this method is a human like skin, that spans from one ellipsoid to another, generating a continuous collision hull (\cref{fig:neo:distance:composed}).
To visualize \acrshort{sdf}s, like the collision hull in \cref{fig:neo:distance:composed}, we used the marching cube implementation of Lewiner et al. \cite{lewiner_2003}.

\subsection{Optimal Control Formulation Adjustments}
The core formulation for \textit{Neo} is the same as for \textit{Morpheus}.
We are keeping the model from \cref{sec:morpheus:model} and use the same controls, states, and dynamics as in \cref{eq:morpheus:dyn,eq:morpheus:constraints:f}.
Analogous to \textit{Morpheus} the overarching goal is to remain in a bipedal stance, while avoiding an approaching object.
\\
The first step is to update the in \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints:dodge} given dodge constraint with one that spans the whole duration.
Together with \cref{eq:neo:distance:ellipsoid,eq:neo:distance:smin} the new constraint
\begin{equation}
  d_\text{safe} \leq d(\bm q)
  \label{eq:neo:constraints:dodge}
\end{equation}
estimates the distance between the collision hull and the approaching object.
As the robot is now tracking the trajectory of the object we are able to move the robot not only into the evasion pose, but also consider the following recovery.
To test this new ability, the objects trajectory is considered to pass the robot at around half distance, so that a combined evasion recovery motion is calculated.
Because of the improved evasion scenario we are also updating the terminal constraint to
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm q - q_\epsilon \\
    - v_\text{stat.}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \leq \bm x_f \leq
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm q + q_\epsilon \\
    v_\text{stat.}
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is based on \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints:terminal}.
Additionally, to solely requiring a quasi-stable end position, we add the initial joint position to the constraint.
\\
The composed objective function is changed to,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\bm x(\cdot),\bm u(\cdot)} \quad
  \int_0^{T_f} \frac 1 2 \left(\omega_q l_q(\bm q)^2 + \omega_r l_r(\bm{u})^2 \right) dt.
\end{equation}
As the motion has to cover evasion and recovery, and is therefore longer, we need to make sure that the solver does not "park" the robot in an unnatural stability optimum.
Instead of primarily optimizing the positioning of the \acrshort{zmp}, we hold the robot as long as possible in the initial pose, to mimick a robot working on a task, and initiating the evasion in the last possible moment.
The consequence is the removal of the stability $l_s$ and head term $m_h$ in favor of a new joint pose Lagrange term:
\begin{equation}
    l_q(\bm x) = \frac{\| \bm q-\bm q_0 \|^2_2}{n_q \cdot \bm{q}_\text{exp}^2}
    \label{eq:neo:obj:joint}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Resulting Motion} \label{sec:neo:result}
\Cref{fig:neo:results:banner} shows a motion that was generated for a head targeted approaching object.
The object was launched \SI{0.7}{\m} away from the robots head with a velocity of \SI{0.5}{\m \per \s}.
Its trajectory is not perturbed by gravity and therefore remains horizontal.
As we want to test the motion generators ability to find a collision avoidance motion containing an evasion and recovering move, the whole duration is raised to $\SI{2.5}{\s}$.
The amount of shooting nodes is raised to $90$.
This is necessary as the constraints solely apply in between intervals.
A sparse shooting gird what lead to possibly ignored collisions during the forward simulation.
\\
For the terminal pose we define an allowed joint angle offset of $\SI{0.1}{\radian}$.
The newly introduced pose term (\cref{eq:neo:obj:joint}) of the objective function is weighted with $\omega_q = 10^{-6}$ to overshadow the regularization term \cref{eq:morpheus:obj:regularization}.
\Cref{tab:neo:variables} lists the above described changes to the earlier described \textit{Morpheus} setup (\cref{tab:morpheus:variables}).

\par
Our first step is to walk through the motion sequence and analyze the calculated solution.
As the generator optimizes for keeping the initial pose as long as possible the robot remains in an upright position until the fourth image.
However, if we look at second and third image with the corresponding joint angles in \cref{fig:neo:results:graphs} at around $\SI{0.5}{\s}$, we find a change in joint angles.
It seems that the robot lowers its torso, to move into a more torque efficient pose and prepare for the following swing up motion.
\Cref{fig:neo:results:graphs:leftAnkle,fig:neo:results:graphs:leftKnee} show a substantial temporal dip in torques, for the ankle and knee joint.
To counter this, the hip joint angle needs to be slightly increased (\cref{fig:neo:results:graphs:leftHip}).
\\
Shortly before the object reaches the head, the robot is propelled back up and into a lean over pose.
While the previous torque minimization was likely caused by the objective regularization term \cref{eq:morpheus:obj:regularization}, we did not include any initiative for leaning over.
The fifth image in \cref{fig:morpheus:results:banner} displays the lean over part of the motion.
The H1-2 is pushed to its limits, regarding the ankle and knee hardware limitations (\cref{fig:neo:results:graphs:leftKnee,fig:neo:results:graphs:leftAnkle}), as well as the \acrshort{zmp} position.
\\
At this point the object is approximately above the robots root.
To avoid the object the robot squats underneath the object.
It can be seen that \textit{Neo} pushes during the squat to the absolute limit of the H1-2 hardware capabilities, using as much of the allowed hip and ankle torque as possible (\cref{fig:neo:results:graphs:leftKnee,fig:neo:results:graphs:leftAnkle}).
\\
Now that the object fully passed the robot, it recovers back into the initial pose.
The torque profiles show, that most of the torques are coming from the ankle joints (\cref{fig:neo:results:graphs:leftAnkle,fig:neo:results:graphs:rightAnkle}).
An explanation for the high torques at that point could be the slow recovery motion, as it drags on for roughly $\SI{0.7}{\s}$.
\par
Through out the motion it is apparent, that the motion generator successfully explores the capabilities of the H1-2, pushing it to the in \cref{eq:morpheus:constraints:joint} defined hardware limitations.
The \acrshort{zmp} for example is pushed to the bounds of our reduced \acrshort{pos} during the lean phase at around $\SI{1.4}{\s}$.
Also, the knee joints range of motion as well as available is also fully used for the squatting part of this specific motion.
Compared to the previous motion, this one not only uses high torques in the ankles, but also for the knee joints.
This results in a lower squat and more dynamic motion overall.
\\
As the stability constraint was removed from the objective function and substituted by a joint pose goal, the solver is not forced towards solely centered \acrshort{zmp} solutions.
This possibly allows the \textit{Neo} method to explore more difficult motions than \textit{Morpheus} could.
\par
We showed that the \textit{Neo} motion generator is capable of finding \acrshort{zmp} stable evasion motions for horizontally approaching objects.
It uses the abilities of the provided hardware to its maximum, but also cuts corners to optimize motions for torque minimization in phases were this is possible.
An example is the gathering of momentum for the first seconds of the motion, before the lean up occurs.
\\
To further investigate the limitations of this method robot combination, a multitude of different object trajectories has to be tested.
This will be especially interesting for angled trajectories, which can be avoided by small upper body perturbation.
An implementation for such a test setup is provided in the following section.
